How to Price Freelance Work When Your Income Changes Every Month

FromOmni Consumer Concepts
ToIndependent workers
SubjectPricing freelance work

Most freelancers underprice for years without realizing it. Not by a little, and not because they lack talent. They just never ran the math on what their rates actually need to cover. This guide walks through how to price freelance work so a slow month is uncomfortable at worst, not a crisis. The examples use made-up numbers to show the method. Plug in your own and the answer changes, which is the point.

If you are brand new to this, the budgeting method behind the numbers is covered in our variable income budget guide. Pricing is the income side of the same coin.

The Slow-Month Test

Here is the pricing question that matters: can your rates carry you through a slow month without panic? Not your best month. A normal thin one.

Work it backwards from what it costs to keep your life running. As an example: say your monthly costs land around $2,500, and in a slow month you can realistically bill 60 hours (the rest goes to finding work, admin, and waiting on replies). Your floor rate is $2,500 divided by 60, roughly $42 an hour. Below that rate, a slow month is not just tight. The math does not work.

That number is your floor, not your price. Your actual rates have to cover more than the bills: the tax slice you set aside from every payment (our freelancer tax savings guide covers that habit), slow periods, unpaid admin time, equipment and software, and the fact that you will never bill 100 percent of your hours. Freelancers who do this math for the first time often find they have been pricing well below what the numbers require. That is not a judgment. The math was just never done.

Hourly, Project, or Retainer? Run a Mix

Each pricing model has a place:

Hourly is honest and simple, but it caps your income at your hours and punishes efficiency. It fits undefined scopes, consulting, and work where the client controls the timeline.

Project pricing pays for the outcome, not the hours. Get faster and you earn more per hour without raising rates. It fits defined deliverables and repeatable work. The risk is scope creep, so define the scope in writing and price revisions separately.

Retainers are the steadiest tool a freelancer has when income changes every month. A client pays a fixed amount monthly for ongoing availability or output. Even one or two small retainers turn part of your income into something you can count on. They fit maintenance, ongoing content, and regular design or dev work.

The strongest freelance setups usually run a mix: retainers for the base, projects for the upside, hourly only when the scope genuinely cannot be fixed.

Deposits Change Everything

Asking for money up front does three things: it filters out unserious clients, it improves your cash flow timing, and it makes cancellations far less painful. Asking for 50 percent upfront on project work is a common example, not a rule. Even 25 or 30 percent changes the dynamic completely. If a client balks at any deposit at all, that tells you something worth knowing before you start.

This matters double when income changes every month, because a deposit arriving this week can be the difference between a comfortable month and a scramble. Your payment terms are part of your pricing.

Raising Your Rates: The Practical Version

If your math says you are underpriced, raise rates on new clients first. No grand announcement needed. Quote the new number on the next proposal and watch what happens. Many freelancers find the market absorbs an increase of 10 to 20 percent with barely a ripple, which says a lot about how much money was being left on the table. Those percentages are examples, not targets. Your market sets the real number.

For existing clients, give notice and keep it simple: "Starting next quarter, my rate is moving to X. I wanted you to hear it from me first." Some accept, some negotiate, a few leave. The ones who leave at a fair rate were usually the ones keeping you overworked and underpaid.

One timing note: raise rates when you have work, not when you are desperate. Desperation pricing and confident pricing quote different numbers for the same work.

Price on the Annual Number, Manage Cash Monthly

Stop evaluating your income month by month. A $900 April feels like failure until you see the full year. Price and plan on the annual number. Manage the cash flow month by month with a system built for income that changes every month: budget off your lowest normal month, give every surplus dollar a job, and run a short monthly routine so nothing sneaks up on you.

The months are weather. The year is climate. Your pricing is what makes the climate livable.

Run the Numbers Once, Then Keep Them Honest

Do the slow-month test this week: your monthly costs divided by realistic slow-month billable hours. Compare the result to your current rate. If there is a gap, that gap is your pricing project for the next few proposals.
