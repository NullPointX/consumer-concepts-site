How Much Should Freelancers Save for Taxes?

FromOmni Consumer Concepts
ToIndependent workers
SubjectSetting money aside for taxes

When you work a regular job, taxes disappear from your paycheck before you ever see the money. When you freelance, every dollar arrives in full — and the tax bill arrives later, all at once. That surprise is one of the most common ways freelancers end up in debt.

The fix isn't complicated. It's a habit: set aside a slice of every payment the day it arrives, and pay the IRS in quarterly installments instead of one painful lump sum. Here's how it works.

Quick disclaimer: This is general information, not tax advice. Tax rules depend on your state, your income level, and your situation. When in doubt, talk to a tax pro — a one-hour consultation is cheap insurance against an expensive mistake.

Why Freelancers Owe More Than They Expect

Employees split payroll taxes with their employer. Freelancers pay both halves. On top of regular income tax, you owe self-employment tax — 15.3% on your net earnings, covering Social Security and Medicare.

That 15.3% is the part that blindsides people. Your income tax might be modest, but self-employment tax applies from the first dollar of profit. This is why the amount you need to set aside is higher than most new freelancers guess.

So How Much Should You Set Aside?

There's no single correct percentage — it depends on your total income, your state, and your deductions. But you need a starting number, so here's how to think about it:

  • A common starting point is 25–30% of profit (income minus business expenses). For many freelancers in an average tax bracket, this covers income tax plus self-employment tax with a little cushion.
  • If your income is on the lower side, you might land closer to 20–25%.
  • If you're in a high-tax state or a higher bracket, 30–35% is safer.

One approach: start at 30% and adjust after your first full tax year, when you see your actual effective rate. It's better to over-save slightly and get a refund than to under-save and owe money you already spent.

The key word is profit, not revenue. If a client pays you $5,000 but you spent $1,000 on subcontractors and software to earn it, you set aside your percentage of $4,000 — the profit. Track your expenses; they directly shrink the amount you owe.

The Separate-Account Habit (The Part That Actually Matters)

The percentage matters less than the habit. Money that sits in your main account feels spendable, and "I'll set it aside later" turns into "I'll figure it out in April." Here's the routine:

  1. Open a separate savings account labeled "Taxes." Any free savings account works.
  2. Every time a client pays you, move your percentage the same day. Not at the end of the week. Not at the end of the month. The day it arrives.
  3. Never touch it except to pay the IRS. Treat it like it isn't your money — because it isn't.

This one habit eliminates the April panic entirely. The money is already there, already separated, already accounted for. Tax season becomes paperwork instead of a crisis.

Quarterly Estimated Taxes, Explained Simply

The IRS doesn't want to wait until April for its money. If you expect to owe $1,000 or more in tax for the year, you're generally required to pay in quarterly estimated installments. Miss them and you can face an underpayment penalty — essentially interest on the tax you should have paid earlier.

The deadlines, every year:

  • January 15 — for September through December income
  • April 15 — for January through March income
  • June 15 — for April through May income
  • September 15 — for June through August income

(When a date falls on a weekend or holiday, it shifts to the next business day.)

You can pay online through IRS Direct Pay or the Electronic Federal Tax Payment System (EFTPS). Your state likely has its own quarterly system too — check your state's tax site, since state estimated payments are separate from federal ones.

The Safe-Harbor Rule: How to Avoid Penalties Without Predicting the Future

Here's the problem with quarterly payments when your income changes every month: how do you know how much to pay if you don't know what you'll earn this year?

The IRS gives you a way out, called the safe harbor. You won't owe an underpayment penalty if your quarterly payments add up to at least:

  • 90% of this year's total tax, or
  • 100% of last year's total tax (110% if your adjusted gross income was over $150,000).

That second option is the practical one for freelancers. You know last year's tax bill — it's a fixed, known number. Divide it by four, pay that each quarter, and you're penalty-proof even if this year turns out wildly different. If you earn more this year, you'll still owe the difference in April — but no penalty on top of it.

New freelancer with no "last year" yet? Then aim for the 90%-of-this-year target using your best estimate, and adjust each quarter as your actual income comes into focus. This is another reason the separate-account habit matters: if you've been setting aside 25–30% all along, the quarterly payments are just transfers, not scrambles.

Deductions: The Other Half of the Equation

Everything above is about what you owe. Deductions shrink that number, and freelancers miss them constantly. Common ones include:

  • Home office (simplified or actual method)
  • Software, subscriptions, and tools you use for work
  • Phone and internet (business-use portion)
  • Health insurance premiums (if self-employed)
  • Retirement contributions (SEP-IRA, Solo 401(k))
  • Mileage and business travel

You don't need to become a tax expert — you just need to track expenses as they happen. A shoebox of receipts in April is how deductions get forgotten. A running log takes seconds per expense and pays for itself at tax time.

Your Tax Routine, Start to Finish

  1. Daily: When a client pays, move your set-aside percentage to the tax account the same day.
  2. Ongoing: Log every business expense as it happens.
  3. Quarterly: Pay your estimated installment by the deadline (Jan 15, Apr 15, Jun 15, Sep 15).
  4. Yearly: File your return, compare what you set aside against what you actually owed, and adjust your percentage for next year.
  5. When unsure: Talk to a tax pro. Seriously — especially in your first year, or if your income jumps significantly.
